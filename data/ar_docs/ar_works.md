In her introductory remarks, editor Zsuzsa Lőrincz discusses the two great Indian epics, the _Ramayana_ and the _Mahabharata_. Zsuzsa Lőrincz explains the framing story of the _Mahabharata_, set in the first half of the first millennium BC: the struggle for power between two families, the Pandavas and the Kauravas. She discusses the length of the epic, its characters, figures from Indian mythology and the story of Savitri.

King Asvapati and Queen Malavi are deeply saddened because they have no heir. They decide to make an expiatory sacrifice to Queen Savitri, during which the king would even sacrifice himself. During the ceremony Queen Savitri comes to life and, through her dance, promises that they will be blessed with a child.

The child grows up and bears an uncanny resemblance to the goddess, but cannot speak and expresses all her emotions through dance. Savitri falls in love with Prince Satyavan and marries him, despite Narada, the heavenly sage, warning her of ominous signs.

After a year, Yama, god of the dead, takes Satyavan's soul. Through her dance Savitri softens Yama's heart. He cannot return her husband's soul, but accompanies her and helps her on her journey into the underworld.

Savitri visits the gods one after another to plead for the return of her husband's soul. She visits Agni, god of fire; Kadru, mother of serpents; Vinata, mother of birds; then Garuda, the man-bird, flies her up to the gods.

Savitri's fidelity softens the hearts of the gods. She is helped by Varuna, god of the waters; Putana, the giant woman; Samvarana, guardian of the Earth; and finally the god Brahma directs her to Vishnu. Vishnu gives her advice, and Savitri returns home and brings Satyavan back to life.

The story is based on Southeast Asian motifs. The high official cries out for help, but the doorkeepers find their master already dead in a room secured from the inside with chains.

The investigator finds the case extremely mysterious, since the room can be approached only through this one point. There is no trace of the murderer, and the guards saw no one either enter or leave.

The questioning of the household begins. The high official's wife, who is being kept in a cage, is also brought before the investigator, and she tells a strange story.

The woman had arrived at the palace with her former husband, the scribe, who sought the nobleman's favour in the hope of obtaining a position. The couple brought their host an unusual gift: a magic mirror. The mirror shows everyone what they desire most deeply.

The high official immediately tried it, and what appeared frightened the scribe most of all. It turned out that the host had set his eyes on his guest's wife.

They began playing dice, and because the guest lost repeatedly, the high official demanded the attractive woman in exchange. The couple strongly protested, so the host had the scribe beheaded and his wife locked in a cage.

From then on the evil man lived in constant fear. Despite every precaution, the ghost of the beheaded scribe gave him no peace.

One day, when he looked into the magic mirror, his justice-dealing self looked back at him. At the call of his opposite, he stepped into the mirror of imagination and, passing through the corridors of memory, came face to face with his evil deeds.

When he reached the room of his pure self, they began playing dice. Because he lost the game, he was forced, as punishment, to stand before a tribunal. Because of his sins he could not receive absolution, and his conscience-self sentenced him to suicide.

After hearing the story, the investigator realises that there can be no other explanation for the case and awards all of the high official's property to the widow of the scribe.

### Contents

Oceania, this distant continent, in terms of its culture, worldview, system of customs, way of thinking and philosophy, at first sight seems far removed from the European mind.

These myths and legends—isolated and free from the influence of other cultures—survived for centuries through oral tradition and preserved intact the ancient spiritual heritage of the people who lived there.

The television play, made using ancient Oceanian folk tales, tells the love story of Kāma, the Rainbow Girl, and Maui, the Man, and their fierce struggle against the elements and the gods.

As the events unfold, beyond a kind of creation myth of the world, the power of Man also slowly takes shape before us: Man was created by a god, yet stands outside divine laws.

Thunder wishes to marry. His choice falls upon Kāma, the Rainbow Girl.

The Lady of the Mountains becomes enraged because she was not chosen and plots revenge. As her instrument she chooses Maui, the Man, whom she tempts with a magic bow, thereby setting events in motion.

The Man cannot resist and shoots the arrow, although the Supreme God warns him that by his action he may call down fate upon himself.

Hearing the whistling of the arrow, Maui regrets what he has done and sets out to put things right.

The arrow embeds itself in the wall of Kāma the Rainbow Girl's house. The young pair fall in love at first sight and cannot conceal it from Thunder, who arrives at that very moment.

In his jealousy, Thunder sacrifices his bride to the Rain God.

This is the moment the Lady of the Mountains has been waiting for, so that she may take what she considers her rightful place beside Thunder.

Maui cannot reconcile himself to the loss of his beloved and decides to bring her back from the realm of the dead.

Gods and forces of nature stand in his way, though at times he also finds helpers.

With every obstacle he overcomes he moves closer to understanding both himself and the world, and in the end he also regains his beloved.
